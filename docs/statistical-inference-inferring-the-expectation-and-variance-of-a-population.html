Behavioral Statistics: Theory and Application

Chapter 4 Statistical Inference: Inferring the Expectation and Variance of a Population

Figure 4.1: It’s finally time for statistics.

In the last few chapters, you have gone on a whirlwind tour, and you should be very proud of the things you have learned. We started at the very basics - discussing sample spaces and the stuff we now call probability. Next we talked about things we call random variables and descriptions of their randomness we call probability distributions. We talked about both continuous and discrete random variables, and importantly, we learned about the expectation and variance of those random variables. At many moments throughout this (perhaps long; although hopefully exciting) adventure you may have asked yourself why all this is so relevant. We are behavioral researchers after all! We aren’t stupendously interested in probability or the concept of randomness. But, as you will see, these concepts will help us unlock a wonderful capacity to study the things that we love to learn about.

It is therefore prudent to return to the very first section of chapter 1 - behavioral research. Suppose are conducting descriptive research of a population of interest. We conduct some sort of measurement with an individual or creature (heretofore also referred to as individuals for simplicity and sympathetic reasons; the author owns two cats) from the population of interest. Via some random process, this individual is selected and we make our measurement. Indeed, if we repeated such an experiment, e.g., selection of an individual via some random process and conducting a measurement, the value of our measure might change from measure to measure. That is to say, we can consider the thing we are measuring as a random variable. That random variable will have some sort of distribution or description of its randomness. However, unlike the previous chapters of this book, we will have absolutely no idea as to the nature of that distribution. We won’t know its form, expectation, and/or variance.

In behavioral research, these random variables and their corresponding probability distributions are referred to as populations. The probability distribution describes all the likely (and unlikely) values within the population of interest. For example, if we were studying the height of cats (it’s Caturday as I write this), we might find that cats who are 1 inch tall are very rare and cats who are about 11 inches tall to be relatively frequent. As such, the population we are interested in is the height of cats and its distribution of likely and unlikely values. Some textbooks and instructors will blur the lines between a population of individuals and a population as I have described above - as a measure and its distribution. That is to say, they will endeavor to use statistics to describe a characteristic of a population of humans (e.g. happiness level or some other metric). For the sake of simplicity we shall refer to populations as a measure/item of interest and its distribution among the individuals we hope to study.

As we will learn later, we will be better able to describe the population we are interested in if we gather more than a single observation of our measure. Simply measuring one cat’s height would be absolutely insufficient to learn about the population of interest, for example. Suppose we called our measure of cat height - \(X\). To get a better sense of the distribution this variable, we would endeavor to randomly seek out multiple cats and measure their height. Throughout this difficult, adorable, and scratchy process, it would be our hope that the random process for selecting cats is truly random - e.g., every single cat or individual has equal likelihood to be selected for measurement. In the case of a random sample like this, we would now have several random variables \(X_1\), \(X_2\), \(X_3\), … , \(X_n\) for the \(n\) cats we hope to measure. These will each have the same probability distribution because they are each from the same population of cat heights. If we further believe/know that these random variables are independent, then we would call the set of random variables independent and identically distributed or i.i.d.

Of course, these are random variables, because as we pondered way back in chapter 1, the actual realized value of each of the above random variables may change from sample to sample. Thus, to reduce some ambiguity, we will again refer to a particular realization of our sample in lower case letters. In this case, we would write \(x_1\), \(x_2\), \(x_3\), …, \(x_n\) to refer to a single outcome of our sampling procedure. Next comes the fun part. In this section we are going to learn about how we might use this sample to make inferences about the population of interest. In particular, we will learn about how we might infer the expectation and variance of the population. As you will see, this starts with describing our sample.
